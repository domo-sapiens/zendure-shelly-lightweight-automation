Inverter efficiency

← back to dashboard

The Zendure app refuses to set an output below 30 W. The hardware tracks accurately down to 10 W, so that floor is a software restriction — but the logged data says it is a well-chosen one. This page shows the measurement, the method, and what it costs in practice.

The loss model

Every sample gives an AC figure the inverter delivered and a DC figure the battery gave up. Plotting one against the other over thousands of samples gives a straight line:

loading… Pdc = DC drawn from the pack · Pac = AC delivered to the house

Two numbers fall out of it, and they mean quite different things:

  • The slope is marginal efficiency: of each extra watt you ask for, this fraction arrives. It is consistently ~95 %, and it barely moves between measurement windows.
  • The intercept is the interesting one. It is a fixed overhead the inverter draws whenever it is converting at all, independent of how much it is delivering. Roughly 18–22 W.

That intercept explains the entire shape of the efficiency curve. Efficiency is not a property of the inverter so much as a consequence of dividing a fixed cost across a variable output:

efficiency(P) = P / (slope × P + overhead) so it collapses as P approaches the overhead, and asymptotically approaches 1/slope as P grows

Why the 30 W floor is defensible

…

Below roughly 30 W the device spends more energy running the inverter than it delivers to the house. A manufacturer refusing to operate there is making a reasonable call on the owner's behalf, even though the hardware is physically capable of it.

Method, and why it is fussier than it looks

Two filters do almost all the work. Without them the result is not merely noisy — it is impossible, producing efficiencies above 100 %.

1. Solar must be zero

packData.power is net pack power. While the sun is charging the pack and the inverter is discharging it, that figure is offset by generation, so dividing AC output by it overstates efficiency. The unfiltered query produced 151 % in the 70–79 W bin, which is what exposed the error. Only periods with no generation measure battery-to-AC conversion.

2. The setpoint must be holding still

The AC and DC readings are not sampled simultaneously. While the setpoint is moving, a sample pairs an AC value from one moment with a DC value from another, and the ratio is meaningless in both directions. Only intervals where outputLimit was unchanged from the previous sample are used.

3. The intercept moves more than the slope

Across measurement windows the slope holds at ~95 % while the overhead estimate ranges about 18–22 W. That is expected rather than worrying: an intercept is extrapolated from where the data is down to zero, so it is sensitive to how much low-output operation happens to be in the window. The curve through the measured range is far better determined than the intercept alone. Treat the overhead as "about 20 W", not a constant of nature.
